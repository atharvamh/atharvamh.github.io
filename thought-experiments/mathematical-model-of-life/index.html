The Mathematics of Life

Atharva Mhetar | 20 October 2026

A life can be modelled as a sequence of states in space and time, linked by choices made under uncertainty. This page formalises that idea, introducing each variable and equation in turn and explaining what it represents.

Life paths through spacetime

Time runs left to right and cannot be reversed. The vertical axis is position. Every life belongs to the same person with the same decision style, who earns more reward at each step the closer they are to the line of highest reward. Only outside influence differs between lives.

  • The life being followed. One possible path from birth to the end of life.
  • Other possible lives. Same person, same decision style, different outside events. Teal lives earn more total reward over their whole course, rose lives earn less.
  • Where reward is highest. Reward R is earned at every step, and is larger the closer the person is to this line. Success J is the total over the whole life.
  • Reachable region. The shaded area covers every place a person could be, since movement per step is limited.
Reward collected at each step

Bars: the reward R the highlighted life earns at each step. It is high when the life is close to the line of highest reward and low when it is far away. Lines: the running total J for every life. Success is where each line ends up after the last step, so a life that gets close early collects more along the way.

β is how sharply the person pursues reward: at 0 they have no aims, at 10 they are close to a strict optimiser. Set outside influence to zero and raise β, and the lives draw together into one: a closed-loop system in which choices alone determine the life.

This simulation is a simplified illustration: one position axis, reward that depends only on distance from the line of highest reward, outside influence as plain random noise, choices made by the soft policy of step 3 from five possible moves, a fixed length of life, and no life variables wk, so options and reward do not change as the life goes on.

The variables

Everything in the model is one of four things: where you are, what you can do, what happens to you, and how you score the result.

Where you are
Sk
Your state at step k
A snapshot of you: place, time, and the life variables below. Steps are k = 0, 1, 2 … n, where n is the step at which the life ends.
x, y, z
Position in space
Where you physically are: your city, your house, your desk.
tk
Time
Moves forward by a fixed slice Δt every step, whatever you choose.
wk
Life variables
A list of numbers describing your life apart from place and time, for example wk = (health, wealth, skills, relationships). They change at every step, and they drive most of the model: your options A, your reward R and your hazard h all depend mainly on wk. Which quantities to include is a modelling choice.
S0
Birth event
(x0, y0, z0, t0, w0): where, when, and with what starting conditions you began.
What you can do
𝒜
Universal action set
Every action anyone could conceivably take.
A(Sk)
Your options right now
The slice of 𝒜 open to you in this state. Enrolling at a university is only in the set if you can reach one and afford it. Set mostly by wk, and also by where you are.
ak
The choice you make
One action picked from A(Sk).
π
Your policy
π(a | Sk): your decision style, the probability of picking each available action in a situation. Cautious, bold, impulsive, planned. People rarely choose identically from identical situations.
What happens to you
εk
Everything not your choice
Luck, the economy, accidents, other people’s decisions. It is an input to the dynamics, not an error bar. Drawn from p(εk | Sk, ak), because your state and choices change what kind of events reach you. Its fresh, unpredictable part is treated as independent from step to step. Lasting outside factors, such as the economy or a long relationship, belong in wk instead.
f
The dynamics
The rule of the world: given where you are, what you chose, and what the outside world did, it says where you land.
h
Hazard: the chance the life ends
h(Sk, ak): the probability that life ends at step k, given that it has lasted until then. It depends on your state and your choice, such as your health, how risky your surroundings are, and what you do. Outside events (ε) are averaged out, so h is the overall chance, not a single roll. A higher h means a shorter expected life.
How you score it
Ri
Person i’s reward function
A personal definition of success. For one person it rewards wealth, for another peace with family, for another following a passion. Success depends on the frame of reference of the person measuring it.
γ
A life path
The whole sequence (S0, S1, … Sn).
Ji(γ)
Success of a life
Add up the reward at every step, weighted by δ, as person i measures it.
δ
Discount factor
How much later reward counts compared with reward now, between 0 and 1. At δ = 1 every step counts equally, as in the graphs above. It measures impatience, since the risk of not living to see a reward is handled by h.

The equations, in plain words

1. Where you start

S0 = (x0, y0, z0, t0, w0)

Birth is the first point of the path. You do not pick it. It fixes your starting place, time and resources.

2. Time always moves forward

tk+1 = tk + Δt

Time is not a choice. The clock ticks one slice per step. Your choices steer everything except this.

3. You can’t outrun light

| rk+1 − rk | < c Δt

Per step, you can only move a limited distance: less than light travels in the same time, and in practice far less. This keeps the path “timelike”, the kind of path a real person can follow. It is the shaded cone in the plot above.

4. Which choices are open

ak ∈ A(Sk) ⊆ 𝒜

You choose, but only from what is available. Each choice also changes your state, which reshapes the options available next time.

5. Your decision style

ak ~ π( · | Sk )

The same situation can lead two people, or one person on two days, to different choices. A policy gives odds to each available option instead of fixing one. It gives zero probability to any action outside A(Sk), so it only ever picks from options that are actually open.

6. One step of life

Sk+1 = f( Sk, ak, εk )

Next state = where you are, plus what you chose, plus what the world did. Two people with identical state and identical choice can land in different places because ε differs. Set every ε to zero and you recover the pure “my choices decide everything” picture.

wk+1 = g( wk, ak, εk )

The life variables are part of the state, so the dynamics update them too. A course raises skills, an illness lowers health, a promotion raises wealth, a move changes relationships. g is the part of f that does this, and it is where most of a life’s change happens.

εk ~ p( · | Sk, ak )

Your state and choices change the odds of what reaches you, not the fact that something does. A strong network or a safe city shifts which events come your way. Given your state and choice, ε is drawn fresh at each step. That independence is what makes the next state depend only on the current state and choice, the Markov property. It holds only if lasting outside factors, such as the state of the economy or a long relationship, are part of wk, leaving just the fresh part in ε.

7. A whole life

γ = ( S0, S1, … , Sn )

A life is the chain of states, ending at a random step n. Because of ε and the chance of ending h, one person does not have one life path but a spread of possible ones.

8. How long a life lasts

P( n ≥ k | γ ) = Πj < k ( 1 − h(Sj, aj) )

Survival probability: the chance of still being alive at step k. At each earlier step the life continues with chance 1 − h, so you multiply those chances together. The bar | γ means “given this path”: h depends on the states and choices along the way, so the answer is for one particular path.

P( n = k | γ ) = P( n ≥ k | γ ) · h(Sk, ak)

The chance that the life ends exactly at step k: survive until step k, then end there with chance h. Added over every k, these chances come to 1, since every life ends at some step. For example, with h = 0.1 at every step, the chances of surviving to steps 0, 1, 2 are 1, 0.9, 0.81, and the chances of ending at those steps are 0.1, 0.09, 0.081. A risky road, a dangerous job or poor health raise h at those steps, and good health and safe surroundings lower it. So your choices shift how long a life is likely to be without fixing it.

9. How successful was it?

Ji(γ) = Σk=0n δk Ri( Sk, ak )

Success is personal. The same path scores high under one reward function and low under another. Nothing in the maths says which Ri is right.

J = R0 + δ R1 + δ2 R2 + δ3 R3 + …

The discount factor δ says how much a reward counts depending on when it arrives. It is a number between 0 and 1, and a reward k steps away is multiplied by δk. With δ = 0.9, a reward counts 1 now, 0.9 at the next step, 0.81 the step after, and about 0.35 ten steps later. At δ = 1 every step counts equally, as in the graphs above. A δ close to 1 describes a patient person who will study or save for years, and a small δ describes someone who mostly values the next few steps. Like Ri, δ is personal: two people with the same reward function but different δ can make opposite choices. Here δ means pure impatience. The chance that life ends before a reward arrives is already handled by the hazard h, so δ should not count that risk a second time.

Revisit, not loop

Revisit: allowed Same place, later time: (xa, ya, za, tc) with tc > ta. You are back at the old house, but as a different person.
Loop: impossible Same place and same time: (xa, ya, za, ta) twice. Time only climbs, so an event never repeats.

Your spatial path can cross itself many times, like a tangled thread on the floor. In (x, y, z, t), and certainly in (x, y, z, t, w), it never touches itself: it is a curve that keeps climbing the time axis.

Optionality

Some choices keep many doors open, others close them. The simplest measure is to count the options:

Optionality(Sk) = | A(Sk) |

Larger means more freedom next step. Good early choices often matter because they keep this large.

H(Sk) = − Σa ∈ A(Sk) π(a | Sk) log π(a | Sk)

The count treats every option as equally real. Entropy weighs them by how likely you are to take them. eH is the effective number of options: it equals | A(Sk) | when all options are equally attractive, and shrinks towards 1 when one option dominates. Ten open doors you would never walk through count for little. A more rigorous measure, empowerment, looks at how many different futures your choices can reach.

The length of a life

The end of life, n, is random: at every step the life ends with probability h(Sk, ak), the hazard from equation 8. Your choices shift this probability but do not fix it. Someone who is on a certain road is there by choice, yet most people who make the same choice arrive safely, and outside events decide the rest.

The path integral

So far the model follows one life at a time. The path integral looks at every possible life at once and asks how likely each one is.

Step 1. A baseline life with no aims

γ = ( S0, a0, S1, a1, … , Sn )

To weigh whole lives, a path records the choices as well as the states.

π0( a | Sk ) = 1 / | A(Sk) |

The baseline person chooses with no preference, picking evenly among the options that are open.

P0(γ) = Πk π0( ak | Sk ) · p( Sk+1 | Sk, ak ) · P( n | γ )

The probability of an entire life under aimless choosing. At each step, multiply the chance of the choice by the chance that outside events lead to the next state. Then multiply by the chance of the life ending when it does, from equation 8. P0 answers one question: how likely is this exact life for someone with no aims at all?

Step 2. Weighting lives by reward

Pβ(γ) = ( 1 / Z ) · P0(γ) · eβ J(γ)
Z = Σγ P0(γ) · eβ J(γ)

Lives that earn more reward become more likely. Z adds this up over every possible life, which is what makes it a path integral: a sum over paths, or an integral when states vary continuously. Dividing by Z makes the probabilities add up to 1.

β sets how sharply a person pursues their reward. At β = 0 the weighting disappears and the life drifts like the baseline. As β grows, high-reward lives dominate, and as β → ∞ only the single best life remains: a strict optimiser. In between, lives are mostly good, with room for variation.

“Going with the flow” can mean three different things here. At β = 0 with the even baseline, a person is truly aimless: indifferent among their options, with circumstances shaping their life. If the baseline is instead “what people around me usually do”, β = 0 means following convention or habit exactly. And someone who deliberately chooses a relaxed life has β > 0, with a reward Ri that values ease. From outside they can look alike, but the model tells them apart.

Pβ = argmaxP [ EP[ J ] − ( 1 / β ) · KL( P ‖ P0 ) ]

The weighting is not arbitrary. It is the best possible trade-off between earning reward and staying close to the baseline. KL, the Kullback–Leibler divergence, measures how far a way of living departs from the baseline, and 1/β sets the price of departing.

A caution: tilt the choices, not the luck

Weighting whole paths also reweights outside events, as if a person could choose their luck. That is valid only when the world is fully controllable. With real outside influence, the tilt is applied to choices alone, one step at a time:

π( a | Sk ) ∝ π0( a | Sk ) · eβ Q(Sk, a)

Q(S, a) is the future reward expected from choosing a in state S, in a softened form defined in step 3. Choices lean towards reward, while outside events keep their own odds.

Step 3. Working backwards from the end of life

To choose well now, a person needs to know what each choice leads to. That depends on later choices, so the values are worked out backwards, from the end of life to birth.

Q(S, a) = R(S, a) + δ · ( 1 − h(S, a) ) · E[ V(S′) ]

The value of a choice: the reward earned now, plus the value of wherever you land next. Later value counts a little less (δ), only arrives if you survive the step (1 − h), and is averaged over outside events (E[ ]), so luck is averaged, not chosen.

V(S) = ( 1 / β ) · log Σa π0( a | S ) · eβ Q(S, a)

The value of a state is a soft maximum over the options. As β → ∞ it becomes maxa Q(S, a), the classic Bellman equation of a perfect optimiser. As β → 0 it becomes the plain average of Q under the baseline. At the end of life V = 0, and from there V is computed one step back at a time.

π( a | S ) = π0( a | S ) · eβ ( Q(S, a) − V(S) )

The resulting policy: options worth more than the state’s overall value become more likely, worse ones less likely, and β sets how strongly. This is the policy used by the simulation at the top of the page, where the pursuit slider sets β.

The most probable life

γ* = argmaxγ log Pπ(γ)

With outside influence there is no single destined life, but there is a most probable one. log Pπ(γ) adds up, along the path, how likely each choice was, how likely each outside event was, and how likely the life was to end when it did.

A(γ) = Σk | Δxk − drift |2 / ( 2σ2 ) − β J(γ)

This is where the physics returns. A particle follows the path of least action, and here −log P plays the role of the action. In the simplest case, where choices and Gaussian outside events with spread σ push on position in the same way, the most probable life minimises A(γ). The first term is the cost of departing from where the world would push you, and the second is the reward gained by doing so. At β = 0 the most probable life is the most typical drift. At large β it tracks the best route, and outside events are the only source of variation. When σ is large, actual lives spread widely around it, so it tells you less.

Possible extensions

The model above is complete in structure, but the simulation uses only part of it. Three extensions would bring the rest to life. Each one changes what a good life looks like inside the model.

1. Life variables that change over a life

wk+1 = g( wk, ak, εk ),   wk = ( health, wealth, skills, relationships )

Choices would build or spend these quantities over time. Studying raises skills, working raises wealth, time with people deepens relationships, and neglect or illness lowers health.

A(Sk) = A( rk, wk ),   Ri(Sk, ak) = Σj θij · uj( wk )

Options and reward would then depend on the life lived so far. New skills open options that were closed, and wealth makes some choices affordable. Reward becomes a weighted mix of how each life variable feels (uj), and the weights θi describe what person i values. This turns “success is personal” into numbers: one person weights wealth, another relationships. The interesting trade-offs appear here, such as giving up reward now to build skills that pay later, which is where the discount factor δ starts to matter.

2. Lives that end at different times

h(Sk, ak) rises with age, rises with risky choices, falls with health

The hazard would let lives end early or late, instead of all at the same step. In the graphs, some paths would stop short. Choices would then carry a second kind of value: besides earning reward, they change how long the person is likely to keep earning it. A risky but rewarding life and a careful but longer one could end up with similar totals. Here the difference between δ and h becomes clear: δ is impatience, while h is the real chance of not being there later.

3. Other people as agents: the model becomes a game

Sik+1 = f( Sik, aik, a−ik, εk )

Each person i gets their own state, policy, reward, discount and pursuit. Their next state depends on their own choice and on everyone else’s choices (written −i, “everyone except i”). Other people move out of the outside influence ε, which keeps only what no one chooses.

πi = soft responseβi( π−i )   for every person i

An equilibrium is reached when everyone’s choices fit everyone else’s. Each person makes the soft, reward-leaning choices of step 3, given what they expect the others to do, and those expectations turn out to be right. For a single choice this is the quantal response equilibrium of McKelvey and Palfrey, and as every β grows it becomes the Nash equilibrium of classical game theory.

“Game” here means shared outcomes, not that life is trivial or about winning. Many such games are cooperative: friendship, family, teams and trade mostly reward both sides. A reward can also include other people’s wellbeing, as a parent’s includes their child’s. This is where questions about trust, reputation and cooperation live, and it explains “keep your circle good” in the model’s terms: the people around you shape your life variables and the options open to you.

What the model is, and is not

The model gives life’s choices a precise structure: states, options, choices, outside influence, survival and personal reward, linked by equations that fit together. It is a lens rather than a prediction. Its key ingredients, such as each person’s reward Ri, their life variables wk and the dynamics f, are not measured here, so the model cannot say how any particular life will go. Its value is in making the assumptions explicit, so that each one can be questioned, refined or tested.
